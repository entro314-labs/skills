# Executive Function Strategies

Organized by function. Each is a coaching strategy; evidence strength varies (see the last section).

## Initiation
- Behavior precedes motivation: commit to 2 minutes with explicit permission to stop (behavioral activation logic). Most people continue; the point is that stopping is allowed.
- Micro-step: smaller than feels reasonable ("open the doc"). Step 1 should be "almost silly".
- Entry points: one sentence; open and look for 60 seconds; list 3 things you don't need to do; do the easy part first; 5-minute promise.
- Stuck diagnostics, one at a time: smallest component; 2-minute version; pre-mortem; delegation check; energy match.
- Body doubling or a shared co-working call for tasks that stay stuck.
- Temptation bundling and a reward chosen in advance.

## Working memory
- Total externalization: capture the moment a thought arises; write instructions down immediately; lists in one trusted place.
- Checklists even for routine tasks (checklists reduce omissions even for experts).
- Visible cues at the point of action (item by the door, sticky note on the monitor).
- Cap visible steps at 5-6.

## Time perception
- 3x rule: multiply the person's own estimate by about 3 (5 min -> 15-20 min; 30 min -> 1-1.5 h; "a couple of hours" -> half a day). Heuristic from planning-fallacy experience; replace with personal data once they track actuals.
- Visual timers showing time elapsed or remaining as a shape, not just digits.
- Calendar important tasks at their 3x duration.
- Count work blocks, not clock time; anchor to events; multiple alarms before fixed appointments (30, 15, 5 min and at the time).
- Leave transition and buffer time; unbuffered schedules fail first.

## Emotional regulation and dread
- Validate before solving. Name the feeling ("frustrating", "shame", "boredom"), pause, then offer options. Labeling emotions measurably dampens their intensity.
- Self-compassion check: "What would you say to a friend in this situation? Say that to yourself." Harsh self-criticism tends to increase avoidance.
- Shame guardrail: never compare to "what should have been done"; patterns are data.
- Dread tasks: mark them, pair with support (body double, reward, tiny first step), schedule in peak energy, and ask "what am I avoiding about it?" - fear, ambiguity, boredom, and size each need different fixes.
- Use humor only if the person does.

## Decision making
- Pre-decide recurring choices (same breakfast, clothes ready, day themes).
- Limit to 2-3 options; "A or B?" instead of "what do you want?".
- If-then rules (implementation intentions): "If the timer goes off, then I save and stand up." "If an urgent email arrives, then I flag it and don't reply yet." "If I don't know where to start, then I do the smallest thing first." Pre-made decisions remove in-the-moment deliberation.
- Do not ask a paralyzed person to choose; pick for them.

## Transitions
- Acknowledge that transitions are hard; physical buffer of 2-5 min (stand, water, bathroom, stretch) without new stimulation (no social media); cap 15 min.
- Name the next task without starting it; then a when-then link.
- Closing rituals: a physical action that marks an end (close laptop, stand, say "I'm done", change rooms).
- Shutdown ritual: write tomorrow's ONE thing; check tomorrow's calendar; clean one small thing; say "Work is done for today"; physical transition.

When-then examples:
- When coffee is finished -> open the work doc.
- When the timer ends -> save and stand up for 2 minutes.
- When leaving a meeting -> write 3 notes first.
- When opening the phone for no reason -> put it face down and drink water.

## Evidence caveats (do not overstate)
- Well supported in general: implementation intentions (if-then planning), behavioral activation, affect labeling, checklists, planning-fallacy correction, body-doubling as common practice with limited formal study.
- Heuristics with no validated thresholds: the 3x rule, the 25-minute contact rule, migration-rate targets, 30/60-minute abandonment tiers, session lengths. Present as starting points to tune.
- Do not state neurobiological claims (e.g. exact "dopamine" mechanisms for initiation) as settled fact; say "many people with ADHD find".
- Strategies complement, not replace, professional care. Medication decisions belong to a clinician.

## Choosing one strategy quickly
Introduce one strategy at a time; several at once add load and make the system itself something to avoid. If a strategy fails three times, change the strategy or the task size, not the pressure on the person. During overwhelm, shrink the step for them instead of asking what the first step should be.

| Blocker | Try |
|---|---|
| Cannot start | 2-minute rule with permission to stop, shrink the step, co-working session |
| Forgets what comes next | Externalize to a checklist |
| Too many small decisions | Pre-decide |
| Task is dull | Temptation bundling, shorter timer |
| Emotion running the show | Label the feeling, then validate |
| Always late or overbooked | 3x estimates, calendar the task, alarm ladder (30/15/5/at time) |
| Cannot switch off | Closing ritual |
| Fails at the same point repeatedly | If-then rule for that point |
