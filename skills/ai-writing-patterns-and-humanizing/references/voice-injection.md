# Voice injection

Do this only after the generic layer is gone. Voice is built from material the author actually has. Where the profile is docs or technical-blog, stay neutral.

## Constraint

Never invent first-person anecdotes, credentials, experiences, or emotions. "In my experience" is allowed only if the author has that experience. If a stronger sentence needs a real story, insert a placeholder (`[a real example from your work]`) and tell the user. Inserted fake self-correction ("Actually, let me back up...") is itself a tell.

## Diagnose the voice first (about 10 sample sentences)

- Average sentence length: 15 or fewer is punchy, 25 or more is flowing.
- Contractions versus full forms.
- First-person frequency.
- Slang or register markers.
- Humor type: dry, self-deprecating, provocateur, none.
- Reader stance: peer, expert, challenger.
- Repeated signature phrases.
- What the voice conspicuously avoids.

If no sample exists, ask for one rather than guessing.

## Four voice types

| Type | Features | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Direct expert | short sentences, no hedging, conclusion first, "you" throughout | "Fix your onboarding. It's where you're losing them." |
| Thoughtful peer | shows reasoning ("Here's how I think about this"), admits what is unknown, shares the mistake before the lesson, "we" only when a real "we" exists | |
| Provocateur | opens with the unpopular claim, names what people avoid saying, takes the counterargument seriously first, lets an uncomfortable number land | |
| Enthusiastic practitioner | excitement shown through detail, practitioner terms without over-explaining, "The thing is..." as the turn to the real insight | |

## Techniques

- **Opinion and stance:** state what the author thinks. Neutral lists of pros and cons read as machine-made.
- **Mixed feelings, named specifically:** "impressive but also kind of unsettling" beats "this is concerning". "Something unsettling about agents running at 3am" beats an abstract concern.
- **Rhythm patterns:** "Long. Short. Long, long. Short." / "Question? Answer. Proof." / "Claim. Specific example. So what?"
- **Paragraph variety:** a one-sentence paragraph for emphasis; question then answer; a short list of 3-5 parallel items; an aside that shows personality; a confession ("I got this wrong the first time").
- **Personal anecdote (real only):** setup, the unexpected event, the principle.
- **Honest admission:** "We thought X. We were wrong. What actually happened was Y."
- **Contrarian setup:** "Everyone says X. The data shows Y." or "X is mostly true, except when [condition]." Needs real data or a real condition.
- **Permitted looseness:** deliberate fragments, sentences starting with "And" or "But", and comma splices for effect, when the author's natural voice uses them.
- **Symmetry breaking:** sections differ in length by how much the point needs.

## Earned endings

A conclusion must add something: a new angle, an honest admission, an earned call to action, a single next action, a reversal, or a hard cut (state the core truth once, nakedly). Restating the intro is a tell, and so is a formulaic closer ("By following these steps, you'll be well on your way"). Aphoristic closers ("X, not Y" lines, tricolons) become a formula themselves: use one only when it states something specific.

## Voice checklist

- Read aloud, it sounds like someone worth following.
- Every paragraph is distinct in shape.
- At least one unmistakable personality moment.
- Recognizable against the author's or brand's other content.
- At least one thing most writers in the field would not say.
- No claim of experience, emotion or data the author does not have.
